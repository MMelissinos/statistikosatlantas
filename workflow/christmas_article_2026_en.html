Employment, education, cleaner electricity, research, knowledge-intensive services, tourism and health: seven official statistical series show measurable positive change in Greece. They do not erase the country's difficulties, and improvement has not reached every region equally. They do, however, provide evidence-based reasons for optimism at Christmas.

Optimism that can be measured

A positive article can easily become a collection of convenient numbers. To avoid that, we looked for official series with a clear definition, a meaningful time span and an improvement that was not confined to a single volatile year. We also checked whether the direction extended beyond the national average to Greece's 13 regions.

The seven changes below do not form an index and should not be added together. Some cover 25 years, others only three or five. Some are percentages, some absolute counts and one is measured in years of life. Their common feature is simpler: each describes a field in which the latest available data are better than the chosen starting point.

1. Employment recovered from 52.9% to 71.0%

In 2013, during the long employment crisis, 52.9% of people aged 20–64 in Greece were employed. By 2025 the rate had reached 71.0%, an increase of 18.1 percentage points. Over the same period, unemployment fell from 27.5% to 8.9%.

The improvement was geographically broad: the employment rate rose in all 13 of the 13 regions between 2013 and 2025. That does not mean that the labour market is equally strong everywhere. In 2025 the difference between Crete and Western Macedonia still exceeded 13 percentage points, and an employment rate says nothing by itself about wages, job security, hours worked or whether qualifications are being used.

Even with those limitations, the change is substantial. A much larger share of working-age residents is now employed, while unemployment is less than one third of its 2013 level. The next test is qualitative: whether the rise is accompanied by better pay, stability and opportunities outside the strongest labour markets.

2. The share of tertiary graduates doubled

In 2000, 17.0% of residents aged 25–64 had completed tertiary education. In 2025 the share was 34.9%. The long-term increase amounts to 17.9 percentage points and is visible in every region.

Attica remained far ahead, with more than 44% of residents in this age group holding a tertiary qualification, while several island and western regions remained around 21%–23%. Yet the directional check is unusually clear: all 13 regions improved between 2000 and 2025.

More degrees do not automatically mean that skills are matched with appropriate jobs, or that graduates can remain in the region where they studied. The indicator records educational attainment, not job quality or migration. It nevertheless reflects a deep change in the country's human capital over a generation.

3. Renewables supplied more than half of electricity consumption

Renewable electricity represented 31.3% of gross electricity consumption in 2019. In 2024 the share reached 51.2%, above the EU average of 47.5%. We reproduced the calculation from the Eurostat energy table: 17,344.233 GWh out of 55,421.492 GWh in 2019, and 29,278.3 GWh out of 57,130.5 GWh in 2024.

This is not the same as saying that half of all energy used in Greece is renewable. Electricity is only one part of final energy consumption; transport and heating remain more difficult. Nor does a high annual share eliminate the need for grids, storage and careful siting. It does show that the electricity system changed rapidly in five years.

4. Research spending rose to 1.53% of GDP

Expenditure on research and development rose from 0.97% of GDP in 2015 to 1.53% in 2024. Researchers also increased from 0.98% to 1.23% of total employment.

The regional improvement was broad but not universal: research expenditure increased in 12 of the 13 regions. Central Macedonia spent a much larger share of its economy on research than the South Aegean or Ionian Islands, demonstrating that national progress still rests heavily on particular centres.

Spending measures resources devoted to research, not their quality, commercial impact or social value. The durable test is whether funding produces stable teams, scientific results, new firms and solutions used by businesses and public services—and whether those benefits spread beyond the strongest metropolitan and university regions.

5. Knowledge-intensive services employ a larger share

The share of employment in knowledge-intensive services rose from 35.9% in 2015 to 38.7% in 2025. These activities include services in which tertiary-educated workers make up a high share of employment.

The movement connects with the expansion of education and research, but the three series do not prove a causal chain. A larger graduate population can widen the pool of specialised workers, while research can create demand for skills; the aggregate data do not follow the same people or firms.

This was one of the less geographically uniform improvements. The share rose in 9 regions and fell in 4. The national increase is real, but it is more concentrated than the gains in employment or education.

6. Tourism nights reached 156.3 million

Overnight stays in tourist accommodation rose from 142,940,411 in 2018 to 156,347,283 in 2025. That is an increase of 9.4% from the pre-pandemic comparison year and 17.8% from 2022.

The direction was positive in 12 of the 13 regions between 2018 and 2025. The figure covers hotels, similar establishments, holiday and short-stay accommodation recorded in the official series, and campsites; it should not be read as a complete measure of all short-term letting or of tourism's net benefit.

More nights can support jobs and local income, but they also increase pressure on housing, water, transport and energy in heavily visited areas. The next measure of success is therefore not simply another record: it is a longer season, greater local value added and infrastructure that can sustain the activity.

7. Life expectancy returned to 81.9 years

Life expectancy at birth rose from 80.1 years in 2021 to 81.9 years in 2024, moving above the pre-pandemic level. The number of licensed physicians also rose nationally from 645.27 to 674.26 per 100,000 residents.

Life expectancy improved in all 13 regions between 2021 and 2024. Physicians per 100,000 increased in 10 regions and decreased in 3. The latter ratio needs caution: a falling population can raise a per-capita measure without an equivalent rise in the number of doctors, and the national total does not reveal specialty, public-system availability or distance from care.

Life expectancy is a period measure, not a prediction for a particular newborn. Even so, the recovery of longevity has direct human meaning and is one of the clearest positive changes after the pandemic shock.

How widely was progress shared?

To avoid relying only on national averages, we compared the first and last year used in each section for all 13 regions. Employment, tertiary education and life expectancy improved in 13 out of 13. Research spending and tourism nights increased in 12 out of 13. Physicians per 100,000 improved in 10, and knowledge-intensive services in 9.

This is an unweighted directional check, not a composite regional ranking. It does not prove that every resident benefited and it gives a small region the same count as Attica. Its purpose is narrower: to distinguish national improvements with a broad geographical base from those driven by a more uneven distribution.

Progress is not distributed equally

National improvement can coexist with persistent or widening gaps. In employment, the difference between Crete and Western Macedonia exceeded 13 percentage points in 2025. In tertiary education, Attica was above 44%, while several island and western regions remained near 21%–23%. In research, Central Macedonia devoted a multiple of the share recorded in the South Aegean or Ionian Islands.

That does not cancel progress. It changes the next question: not only whether the national indicator rises, but whether regions starting from the lowest values are catching up. The answer differs by field. Education improved everywhere over 25 years. Knowledge-intensive services grew strongly in some mainland regions but not universally. The national physician ratio increased while it fell in several island regions.

Small regions also show greater year-to-year volatility. One investment, a large hotel, a university unit or the movement of a few hundred workers can visibly alter a rate. Regional figures therefore need to be read alongside longer trends, not as an annual league table.

Three connections behind the seven series

The first connection is between education, research and knowledge-intensive services. More graduates expand the pool of people able to work in specialised activities; higher research spending can create demand for those skills. The evidence here does not demonstrate that one caused the other, but all three long-term series move in a broadly favourable direction.

The second connection concerns work and health. Higher employment can support incomes and social insurance, while job quality affects health. A health system with adequate staff can help more people remain active. National time series cannot isolate these pathways, so their simultaneous improvement is a reason for further research rather than proof of a mechanism.

The third connection is between energy, tourism and regional development. Cleaner electricity can reduce the carbon intensity of activity, while tourist regions have growing needs for grids, water, transport and power. More tourism makes the expansion of renewables more important, but does not guarantee that supporting infrastructure grows at the same pace.

What would show that improvement is durable?

For the labour market, the next test is not only whether employment remains above 70%, but whether regional gaps narrow and gains come with better pay, stability and sufficient hours. For education, the question is whether more graduates find work that uses their knowledge without having to leave their region.

For research and knowledge services, durability means converting spending and skills into stable organisations, useful scientific results and productive applications, with benefits beyond the strongest centres. For energy, it means storage, grids and progress in transport and heating. For tourism, it means a longer season, more local value and protection of resources. For health, it requires tracking doctors by specialty, provider and place rather than relying on a national total.

If later releases of the same series maintain their direction—and if regions with the lowest starting values begin to catch up—the present optimism will have a stronger foundation. If not, the 2026 figures will remain a useful benchmark for identifying where progress stalled.

What these numbers have in common

The seven changes do not come from one common mechanism and cannot be combined into an “optimism index”. Selecting positive series intentionally creates a positive frame and is not a full balance sheet for Greece. The country continues to face low birth rates, housing pressure, regional inequality, weak productivity in parts of the economy and unequal access to services.

There is nevertheless a common thread: progress is more convincing when it appears in several independent official sources and lasts beyond one year. More employment and education, cleaner electricity, more research and knowledge, stronger tourism activity and recovered longevity do not automatically solve Greece's problems. They expand the capabilities available to address them.

That may be the most useful Christmas message in the data. Optimism does not need to ignore difficulty. It can rest on specific changes, acknowledge where they have not reached, and ask that they continue.

How we measured it

We reviewed the stored series of the Statistical Atlas and selected indicators with an official source, an available national time series, a clear definition and a positive change not limited to a random annual fluctuation. For each indicator we used the latest available year and a starting point appropriate to that series; the periods therefore differ.

Changes in percentages are reported in percentage points unless a relative percentage change is stated explicitly. Regional values are used to examine distribution and exceptions, not to rank a “best” region. We neither weighted nor combined the indicators, and we do not claim causal relationships among them.

The deliberate selection of positive changes reflects the article's theme. It is not presented as a complete picture of the economy or society. The full definitions, caveats and regional values remain available in the Statistical Atlas.
