Greek regions that entered the 2010s with a larger share of residents aged 65 and over generally recorded a larger fall in permanent population by 2021. Across the 13 regions, the association between the 2011 share of older residents and population change over the following decade was strongly negative. The result describes a geographical pattern. It does not prove that ageing caused population decline, or that the same forces operated everywhere.

Two different maps that look alike

In 2011, the largest shares of residents aged 65 and over were found in Epirus, at 23.45%, the Peloponnese, at 22.98%, Central Greece, at 22.41%, the North Aegean, at 22.27%, and Thessaly, at 22.02%. At the other end were the South Aegean at 16.05%, Attica at 17.38% and Crete at 17.64%.

Ten years later, the steepest population fall was in Western Macedonia, at −10.26%. Eastern Macedonia and Thrace followed at −7.56%, Central Greece at −7.15%, the Peloponnese at −6.64% and Thessaly at −6.07%. Only the South Aegean, at +6.09%, and Crete, at +0.22%, recorded an increase.

The two rankings are not identical, but they point in the same general direction. Regions with a younger age structure are more often close to stability or growth, while several regions with a larger older population show a sharper decline. The relationship becomes visible when each region is treated as one observation.

The correlation across the 13 regions

The Pearson correlation between the share of people aged 65 and over in 2011 and the change in permanent population in 2011–2021 is r = −0.785. With 13 observations, the corresponding exploratory parametric test gives p ≈ 0.0015. Spearman's rank correlation, which depends on ordering rather than the exact distance between values, is ρ = −0.676, with p ≈ 0.011.

The negative sign means that, within this particular set, a higher initial share of older residents is associated with a larger fall or a smaller rise in population. It does not mean that each additional percentage point of residents aged 65 and over removes a fixed number of people. Nor does it turn the relationship into a forecast for any single region.

Using the age structure at the beginning of the period matters. If the 2021 share is used instead, Pearson's coefficient remains similar, at −0.780, but interpretation is harder because the 2021 age structure is itself partly an outcome of what happened during the decade.

The 13 regions

Regions that do not fit a simple line

Epirus had the highest share of older residents in 2011, but not the largest population decline. Its fall of 5.01% was substantial, yet smaller than those of Western Macedonia, Eastern Macedonia and Thrace, Central Greece, the Peloponnese and Thessaly. The North Aegean also had a high 65+ share, while its population fall was limited to 2.15%.

At the other end, the South Aegean combined the youngest age structure with the largest population increase. Crete also had a relatively low 65+ share and a small increase. Attica, however, began close to them in age structure but lost 0.38% of its population. These departures are precisely why a correlation must not be converted into a rule.

A leave-one-out check shows how much the result depends on each of the 13 observations. When one region is removed in turn, Pearson's coefficient ranges from −0.834 without the North Aegean to −0.645 without the South Aegean. The direction stays negative in every version, although the strength of the relationship is not independent of the composition of this small sample.

Four different regional paths

The overall correlation is easier to understand when the observations are not read as points on a single uniform line. The South Aegean and Crete form a first pattern: both began with among the lowest shares of people aged 65 and over and were the only regions whose population increased. The South Aegean stands out most clearly, with 16.05% aged 65+ and growth of 6.09%; Crete had 17.64% and marginal growth of 0.22%.

Attica is a separate case. It also began with a relatively young structure, at 17.38%, but its population fell slightly, by 0.38%. That near-stability may conceal large movements within the region, between the centre, suburbs and the rest of Attica. A 13-region analysis cannot identify those internal shifts.

A third pattern comprises Western Macedonia, Eastern Macedonia and Thrace, Central Greece, the Peloponnese and Thessaly. They combined 65+ shares close to or above 22% with population falls exceeding 6%, apart from Western Macedonia, whose share was 21.97% but whose decline, 10.26%, was the steepest. These regions strongly support the negative direction of the relationship.

The remaining regions show why no single rule is sufficient. Epirus was the oldest but did not fall the most; the North Aegean was also old but declined much less than mainland regions with similar starting values. The Ionian Islands had an older structure but a relatively small fall. Migration, tourism, urban concentration, universities and access to jobs and services can alter each path.

Why ageing and decline can coexist

A larger older population may be associated with fewer births relative to deaths because age structure affects how many residents are at childbearing ages and how many are at ages with higher mortality. That is a plausible demographic mechanism, but it is not measured directly by this correlation.

The movement of younger people may also raise the older population's share while reducing the total population. When residents of working and family-forming ages leave, the region loses people immediately and its age mix changes. Conversely, the arrival or retention of younger residents can slow both trends.

Economic opportunities, universities, tourism, access to services, distance from major cities and international migration can affect who stays and who leaves. Unless these factors are incorporated into a common model, the data cannot assign an independent causal effect to ageing.

Natural change and migration are not interchangeable

The fall in population between two censuses is the combined outcome of births, deaths and movements into and out of a region, as well as census and estimation differences. A negative natural balance can coexist with a stable total population if migration offsets it. Conversely, a relatively young region can still shrink if out-migration is sufficiently strong.

This distinction matters for policy. A region losing population mainly because deaths exceed births faces a different mix of needs from one losing young adults through migration. The first points strongly towards age structure, health, care and family formation; the second also points towards employment, housing, education and mobility. The present analysis identifies neither component separately for the whole decade.

How strong is the statistical evidence?

Because there are only 13 regions and the South Aegean is an influential observation, we supplemented the standard correlation test with checks that make fewer assumptions. In a reproducible test of 200,000 random permutations, using seed 20261121, only 278 shuffled arrangements produced an absolute correlation at least as large as the observed |r| = 0.7850337. With the usual correction, the two-sided estimate is p ≈ 0.001395.

A 10,000-sample bootstrap check placed the 95% interval for Pearson's correlation at approximately −0.928 to −0.299. The interval is wide, as expected with 13 observations, but it remains on the negative side. This supports the existence of an association in these data while reminding us that its precise strength is uncertain.

Influence diagnostics reach the same balanced conclusion. The South Aegean has Cook's distance of about 0.827 and leverage of about 0.398, so it has substantial influence on the fitted line. Yet removing it does not reverse the result: the correlation remains negative, at approximately −0.645. An influential observation is not automatically an error; here it is a real regional path that the interpretation must acknowledge.

The ecological trap

Every point represents an entire region. The analysis does not compare individual residents and cannot support the claim that an older person “causes” population decline. Such a conclusion would be an ecological fallacy: transferring a collective geographical pattern to the individual level.

Each region also receives equal weight, regardless of population size. The South Aegean and Attica therefore count as one observation each, even though their populations differ greatly. That is appropriate for asking how regional units differ, but it is not an estimate of the experience of the average Greek resident.

Regions are not fully independent. Neighbouring areas share labour markets, services and migration flows. We did not apply a formal test of spatial dependence. The p-values should therefore be read as exploratory evidence, not as a definitive confirmatory result.

What this analysis cannot tell us

The two indicators do not tell us how much of the decade's change came from natural balance and how much from migration. They do not provide the ages of those who left or arrived, or show whether change was concentrated in regional capitals, smaller towns or villages.

We do not control here for income, employment, tourism, geographical isolation or the presence of students. Nor do we examine the country's 52 smaller regional units, within which very different paths may coexist inside the same region. Thirteen observations are enough to reveal a pattern, not to construct a complete causal model.

What the result means for policy

A region with a high 65+ share is not merely an area with an “ageing problem”. It is a place where health care, long-term care, transport and housing have to serve a different age structure. Population decline may simultaneously reduce the local tax base and increase the distance to essential services, especially in smaller settlements.

The analysis also argues against a uniform response. In a region where out-migration dominates, retaining and attracting working-age residents may be central. Where natural decrease is deeply established, accessible health and care services, links between settlements and adaptation of public infrastructure may matter more. Most regions require both, but in different proportions.

Equal regions, unequal populations

The choice to give every region one observation answers a particular question: whether regions with an older starting structure tended to follow a different population path. It does not answer what share of Greece's residents lived under each combination. A population-weighted analysis would be dominated by Attica and Central Macedonia and would describe the experience of residents rather than variation between regional systems.

Neither approach is inherently superior. Equal weighting is useful for territorial policy because a small island region still has to organise health care, transport and schools as a complete administrative area. Population weighting is useful when estimating a national average effect. Mixing the two questions would make the result appear more precise while changing what it means.

Uncertainty is part of the finding

The coefficient −0.785 is the value calculated from these 13 observed regions, not a universal constant. The bootstrap interval shows that plausible values for the association are spread across a broad negative range. The permutation test asks a different question: how often a relationship at least this strong would appear if the observed age shares and population changes were randomly reassigned among regions.

Agreement among Pearson, Spearman, leave-one-out, bootstrap and permutation checks strengthens the descriptive conclusion because the methods respond differently to ranks, individual observations and distributional assumptions. It still does not turn an observational geographical comparison into a causal experiment. Robustness of association and proof of mechanism are different standards.

The next questions to measure

A fuller regional study would separate births and deaths from internal and international migration, examine age-specific flows and repeat the analysis for smaller territorial units. It would also test employment, income, housing costs, university presence, tourism intensity and accessibility of services in the same framework. With only 13 regions, however, adding many variables at once would produce an unstable model; more detailed geography or a longer panel would be needed.

Following the same indicators after the next census would show whether the relationship persists, weakens or changes direction. Annual population estimates can provide earlier signals, but census counts offer a different level of territorial detail and are not perfectly interchangeable with estimates. Consistency in definitions is therefore as important as adding more years.

For readers and policymakers, the practical value of the analysis is therefore diagnostic rather than predictive: it identifies which regional trajectories deserve closer investigation and makes the assumptions behind that investigation explicit.

The useful question is not whether ageing alone explains decline. It does not. The useful question is which combination of age structure, births, deaths and migration produced each regional path, and which services can remain viable under that path. The correlation is a starting point for that investigation, not its conclusion.

How we measured it

We used ELSTAT permanent-population census data for 2011 and 2021. For each of the 13 NUTS 2 regions, we calculated the percentage change in permanent population and compared it with the share of residents aged 65 and over in 2011. Percentages were calculated from the underlying counts before rounding.

We calculated Pearson's linear correlation, Spearman's rank correlation, a leave-one-out sensitivity range, a 10,000-sample bootstrap interval and a two-sided permutation estimate from 200,000 random shuffles. These are descriptive and exploratory checks on a small set of regional observations. They do not establish causality, correct for multiple exploratory comparisons or replace a model incorporating migration, natural change and socioeconomic factors.
